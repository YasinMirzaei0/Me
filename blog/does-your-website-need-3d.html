Does your website need 3D? When motion helps and when it hurts

26 October 2026 · 5 min read · Yasin Mirzaei

Interactive 3D is one of the fastest ways to make a website feel different from the thousand templates around it. It is also one of the easiest ways to make a site slow, heavy and confusing. The question is not “is 3D good?”, but “does it serve this project?”.

When 3D and motion help

  • Portfolios and brand sites. When the goal is to be remembered, a distinctive first impression is part of the product.
  • Explaining something physical or complex. Rotating a product, or showing how parts fit together, can say more than a paragraph of text.
  • Storytelling. Scroll-driven scenes can guide a visitor through a narrative at a pace you control.

When it hurts

  • Content-heavy sites and shops. People who came to read or buy want speed and clarity, not a show.
  • Audiences on older phones. A scene that runs smoothly on a laptop can stutter on a budget device.
  • Effects without a purpose. If you cannot say what an animation helps the visitor do, it is probably decoration.

Rules I follow when I do use 3D

  1. Keep the important content as real HTML text. Search engines and screen readers cannot read pixels in a canvas.
  2. Set a performance budget. Limit the number of particles or polygons and cap the pixel ratio on high-density screens.
  3. Provide a fallback. If WebGL is not available, show a calm static background instead of a blank page.
  4. Respect reduced motion. Visitors who ask their system for less animation should get a version with little or none.
  5. Pause what is not visible. Stop rendering when the tab is hidden or the canvas is off screen to save battery.

Used with restraint, 3D gives a site personality. Used everywhere, it gets in the way of the message. If you are unsure which side your project falls on, send me a short brief and I will tell you honestly.
